# Research questions

## Central question

How do the digital interfaces and underlying civic infrastructure of Bengaluru and Hyderabad influence citizens' ability to access essential public services, particularly across disability, language and cognitive usability barriers?

## Sub-questions and how far this MVP answers them

| Sub-question | Evidence used | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Do official portals meet basic automated WCAG checks? | axe-core, scripted probes | Partly answered for public pages before sign-in |
| Do language options reflect the languages residents grew up speaking? | Census C-16, language-control scan | Partly answered; translation quality untested |
| How many residents does a text-first interface leave without a direct route? | Census PCA | Scale estimated from 2011 data |
| What does official data show about disabled residents? | Census C-20 | Answered for 2011 categories only |
| How does straight-line nearness to facilities compare? | Facility layers, 500 m grid | Answered for position only; this is not access |
| Are listed informal settlements cut off from primary care? | Slum and health layers | Not by distance on these lists; quality and treatment unknown |
| How much do open maps record about physical accessibility? | OSM wheelchair tags | Measures the record, not the buildings |
| What do people actually experience? | None yet | **Open: needs fieldwork** |

## Humanities framing

We read these portals as artefacts of citizenship. An interface embodies assumptions about who a citizen is: which language they read, what device they own, which documents they hold, and whether they can see the screen. Datasets are also artefacts: what gets listed, dated and maintained shows whose access has been made visible. A service can exist in law and on a map and still be out of reach.
